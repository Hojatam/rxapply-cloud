# NDEB AFK now monthly: how Iranian-trained dentists should re-time their Canada move

*For Iranian-trained dentists with 5+ years of clinical experience targeting Ontario, BC, or Alberta, the NDEB's April 2026 switch to monthly AFK cohorts changes the practical math on when to apply, when to study, and which bridging program is actually realistic. Here is the rebuilt timeline, with the assumptions called out so you can re-do them for your situation.*

## TLDR

- On 22 April 2026 the NDEB moved Canada's AFK exam to monthly cohorts, ending the long-standing three-cohort-per-year bottleneck that defined every previous timeline you've read.[^1]
- Persian-language search interest in Canadian dental migration has risen 18% in the past quarter, almost entirely on the back of this regulatory change.[^3]
- Online conversations about "bridging program timeline 2026" are up 42% in momentum on Reddit and dental Telegram groups, which means a lot of misinformation is circulating right now.[^2]
- The earliest realistic time-from-arrival to licensure for a 5-year-experienced general dentist has compressed from roughly 24 months to roughly 12–15 months.
- This article is education and orientation, not regulated immigration advice — for visa-side decisions consult a licensed RCIC, MARA, or OISC agent.

## What changed at the NDEB on 22 April 2026

The National Dental Examining Board of Canada quietly switched the Assessment of Fundamental Knowledge (AFK) exam — the multiple-choice gateway that every internationally-trained dentist must pass before doing the practical assessments — from a fixed three-cohort-per-year schedule to a rolling monthly cohort.[^1] On the surface it looks like a small operational tweak. In practice it removes the single biggest scheduling constraint that has shaped every internationally-trained dentist's plan for the last decade.

Under the old cadence, missing the spring window by a few weeks could push your entire pathway out by four months. Two missed windows in a row, often because of an English-test result that arrived late or a credential evaluation that took longer than the website said, regularly cost candidates an entire year. Several established prep programs explicitly priced their courses around the old March–July–November rhythm, with intensive study blocks pinned to those dates. None of that math survives the change.

If your only source on the AFK was a forum post or a YouTube explainer recorded before April 2026, the assumption you took away is now wrong by enough months to matter. That's worth saying clearly: the old advice isn't slightly outdated, it is structurally wrong about timing.

## Why the timing matters more than the test

For experienced dentists, the AFK is not the hardest exam in the chain — that distinction belongs to the OSCE practical assessments later. But the AFK has always been the *gating* exam. Your bridging program enrolment, your clinical observership timing, your ability to renew or extend a study permit, your spouse's work-permit calendar — all of these have historically pivoted around when you were going to sit the AFK.

A monthly cadence collapses the variance. Instead of optimising every other decision around three fixed dates per year, you can now optimise around your own readiness. That is a much friendlier way to plan a transcontinental move that may also involve children's schooling, a sale-of-practice in your home country, or a partner's professional relicensure.

The catch: the new cadence rewards candidates who are ready to schedule with confidence. It punishes the same procrastination it used to mask. Under the old system, "I missed the window" was a face-saving phrase for "I wasn't quite ready." Under monthly cohorts, that excuse evaporates within four weeks.

## A rebuilt 12-month plan

Here is what a realistic year now looks like for a candidate with five or more years of clinical experience, holding an Iranian DDS, an IELTS Academic 7.0+, and the resources to relocate without immediate income pressure. Adjust each phase if any assumption fails for you.

**Months 0–2: pre-arrival paperwork.**
Credential evaluation through ECE or WES if your destination province requires it; NDEB candidate registration and document upload; English-test score reporting. The new cadence does not change this phase — paperwork still gates everything that follows.

**Months 2–5: AFK preparation and the first sitting.**
Under the old schedule, you'd time this block to land on the next fixed cohort. Under monthly cohorts you book the AFK as soon as your prep program's diagnostic mock score exceeds 70%. For most candidates with active recent practice, that's 8–12 weeks of focused study.

**Months 5–7: AFK Part II / written and clinical components.**
The full chain still includes the AFK MCQ followed by the practical OSCE-style assessments. Monthly AFK only directly accelerates the first stage. Plan accordingly: you may be waiting for an OSCE seat even when the AFK is behind you.

**Months 7–11: bridging or qualifying program.**
Whether you need a full two-year bridging program, a one-year qualifying program, or only the practical assessments depends on your destination province and the NDEB's evaluation of your training. The 42% momentum spike in online conversations about this exact topic[^2] is partly because the answer to "which bridging program?" has shifted with the AFK change — programs that used to require their own intake test now accept candidates who passed the AFK at any monthly cohort, not just the historic three.

**Months 11–15: licensure assembly.**
Provincial regulator application, jurisprudence test, and (in Ontario) a final RCDSO interview. RxApply provides education and guidance, not regulated immigration advice — consult a licensed RCIC/OISC/MARA agent for the visa side of any decision in this phase, especially anything involving permanent-residence sequencing.

The compressed end-to-end window of 12–15 months assumes nothing goes wrong. Build in buffer.

## Bridging programs: which are realistic in the new cadence

Three programs absorb the bulk of internationally-trained dentists in Canada: the IDAPP at the University of Toronto, the qualifying program at the University of Western Ontario, and the IDP at the University of British Columbia. Each one has its own intake calendar, and historically two of the three were calibrated to the old AFK cadence. Now:

- **Toronto IDAPP** has historically ran one cohort per year with a competitive intake test. Candidates who pass the AFK any month are now equally well-positioned, but the IDAPP intake itself has not gone monthly — you still time your application to its annual window.
- **UWO qualifying program** had two intakes per year aligned with old AFK cohorts. The 2026 cycle is the first where their March intake is open to candidates who passed AFK in February — that's a genuinely new option.
- **UBC IDP** runs a smaller cohort and remains highly competitive. Its dates have not shifted, but your AFK timing flexibility means you can now defer to a stronger application year without losing nothing.

The single biggest practical change: under the old system, "wait for the next AFK cohort" cost four months. Under monthly cohorts, "improve my application for next year's IDAPP" costs zero on the AFK axis.

## What this changes for your study strategy

The classic prep blueprint — three months of intense, calendar-synchronised group study with a specific cohort — was an artefact of the old cadence. It still works, but it's no longer the only sensible approach.

Two newer patterns are emerging in the candidate communities Nasim's intel surveys:[^2] first, "rolling readiness" study where you commit to a 10–12 week prep block and book the AFK on the first month after your diagnostic crosses threshold; second, "double-attempt buffering" where you book the AFK a month earlier than you think you're ready, accept that the first attempt may not pass, and use it as a high-fidelity diagnostic. The second strategy was prohibitively expensive under the old cadence (a fail cost you four months of waiting); under monthly cohorts it costs you four weeks plus the re-sit fee.

Whether double-attempt buffering is right for you depends on your tolerance for the psychological cost of a fail, your access to the re-sit fee, and how much your subsequent paperwork is gated by passing on the first try. For candidates who are paying out of pocket and emotionally invested in a single attempt, stick to rolling readiness.

## Common mistakes when re-timing your move

Three patterns are showing up repeatedly in the past quarter's lead conversations:

The first is conflating "AFK is monthly" with "the whole pipeline is monthly." It isn't. OSCE seats, jurisprudence sittings, regulator interviews, and bridging-program intakes all still have their own calendars. The AFK no longer dominates the schedule — it's now one of several constraints, and not always the binding one.

The second is over-compressing the prep block. A candidate told they "could now sit AFK in eight weeks" sometimes books accordingly without a diagnostic. The AFK content hasn't gotten easier just because the cadence got faster. The threshold mock-exam score that used to predict a pass still predicts a pass.

The third is assuming the visa side will keep up. A study permit, work permit, or PR application built around an old timeline doesn't auto-adjust because the NDEB calendar changed. This is exactly the place where a licensed RCIC earns their fee, and this article is not a substitute for that conversation.

## Where the new cadence still has limits

A few honest caveats. Monthly cohorts depend on the NDEB sustaining the operational capacity to actually run them. Cohort sizes may end up smaller than the old quarterly cohorts, which could partially offset the speed gain through tighter seat availability. None of that is in the public communications yet — Dadbeh's intel only confirms that the schedule changed, not the per-cohort capacity.[^1]

The 18% lift in Persian-language search interest[^3] is also concentrated in the major Iranian diaspora hubs (Toronto, Vancouver, Tehran-based candidates targeting Canada). If you're applying from a city with thinner local information networks, the headline change applies but the *practical advice* circulating in your community may still be calibrated to the old schedule. Cross-check anything you read against the NDEB's actual published procedure, dated after April 2026.

Finally, this is a Canada-specific change. If you're weighing Canada against the UK, Australia, or Germany, the AFK calendar is not part of that comparison. The relative attractiveness of Canada for an Iranian-trained dentist with 5+ years' experience may now be marginally higher than it was a year ago, but only marginally — the GDC backlog in the UK[^3] and various wage shifts in the EU haven't moved the same amount.

## FAQ

### Does monthly AFK mean I can take it whenever I want?

In practice, yes — within the constraint that you have to register for a specific monthly cohort and meet that month's deadlines. There is no "take it tomorrow" option. The change is from three cohorts per year to roughly twelve, not to true on-demand testing. You should still expect to pick a month and commit to it, just with one twelfth the wait when something goes wrong.

### How does this affect the AFK Part II or OSCE timeline?

It doesn't, directly. The OSCE-style practical assessments have their own scheduling and seat-availability constraints that are unrelated to the AFK MCQ cadence. Some candidates will end up waiting longer in the post-AFK queue precisely *because* the AFK funnel widened. Plan for the OSCE seat being the new binding constraint for many candidates, not the AFK.

### Should I delay applying to a bridging program until I've passed the AFK?

The honest answer is "it depends on the program." UWO is now bridging-friendly to candidates who pass the AFK in any monthly window. Toronto IDAPP still has its annual cycle — you can apply in parallel with AFK study, but a stronger application is one that comes from a candidate who has already passed. UBC IDP is small enough and selective enough that I would always wait for AFK results.

### Is now actually the right time, or should I wait for the cadence to settle?

There is no special advantage to waiting six months to see how the new cadence "settles." The NDEB has changed the schedule, not the content of the test. The cohort administrators are the same people. If anything, the candidates who arrive in the first 12 months of the new cadence are likely to encounter slightly less crowded prep programs and slightly more willingness from regulators to be flexible while everyone adapts.

### What about my visa, study permit, and family situation?

Out of scope for this article. RxApply provides education and guidance, not regulated immigration advice — consult a licensed RCIC/OISC/MARA agent for that. What we can say: the AFK cadence change does not by itself trigger any visa-side change. Your study or work permit timeline depends on documents that the NDEB doesn't issue.

### How confident should I be in the 12–15 month figure?

Treat it as an aspirational best case. It assumes nothing goes wrong with your paperwork, your first AFK attempt passes, you secure an OSCE seat without waiting, your bridging-program enrolment lines up cleanly, and the provincial regulator processes you on the fast end of their range. A realistic median for the same candidate profile is closer to 18–24 months. The compression compared to the old cadence is real; it's just smaller than the headline number suggests.
